Why does almost every Norwegian know the phrase “there is no shame in turning around”?

The Norwegian mountain code explained: nine sentences born from a deadly Easter

At Easter 1967, 17 people lost their lives in the Norwegian mountains. The same year, a campaign made nine short rules common knowledge. Here is what each of them means in practice.

Updated 18 November 2026

Few texts are as familiar to Norwegians as the mountain code, fjellvettreglene. It hangs on cabin walls and turns up everywhere before Easter. But it is easy to know the rules by heart without thinking about what they ask of you.

The rules come from the Norwegian Trekking Association (DNT) and the Red Cross. According to the encyclopedia Store norske leksikon, the first nine rules were published in 1952, after several deaths in the mountains at Easter two years earlier. Fifteen years later, in 1967, 17 people died in the mountains at Easter. The same year, the campaign “Welcome to the mountains, but take responsibility yourself” began.

In 2016 the rules were rewritten to fit how people use the mountains now, with more summit trips and steep terrain. That is the version in use today.

9 rulesin three groups: three before you leave, two for the pack and four for the trip itself. They are easier to remember that way.

All nine in one minute

Before you leave

The first three rules are about what you do at home. This is where most bad trips can be stopped.

  1. Plan your trip and tell someone where you are going.Make a plan that still works if something takes longer. Tell someone where you are going and when you will be back, so they know where to look.
  2. Adapt the trip to your ability and the conditions.The trip should suit the weakest person in the group and the conditions on the day. A trip that was fine last year can be too long this year.
  3. Pay attention to the weather and avalanche forecasts.Read the forecasts for the area you are going to, and understand what they mean for your route. Choose easier terrain when conditions are demanding.

In the pack

The next two are about what you carry. They apply on short trips too, because that is where people most often go without.

  1. Be prepared for bad weather and cold, even on short trips.Bring extra clothes, food and drink. Mountain weather can turn faster than the forecast said.
  2. Bring what you need to help yourself and others.In winter that means a wind sack, a sleeping pad, a sleeping bag and a shovel, so you can get through a night out.

To see what this means in practice, read about the night you didn't plan and what goes in the pack.

On the way

The last four are about the choices you make while moving. They are the hardest to follow, because they often mean giving up something you were looking forward to.

  1. Choose safe routes. Recognise avalanche terrain and unsafe ice.Learn to see where it is steep enough for avalanches to release, and stay away when you are in doubt.
  2. Use a map and compass. Always know where you are.Follow the map all the way, not just when visibility goes. By then it is too late to work out where you are.
  3. Turn back in time. There is no shame in turning around.Decide in advance when you will turn around, and stick to it. Change the plan long before anyone is exhausted.
  4. Save your energy and seek shelter if necessary.Keep a pace everyone can hold all day. If the storm comes, seek shelter while you still have strength. Read how to dig a snow cave.

Why they still work

None of the rules is about gear you have to buy or skills you must have. They are about making decisions early, while you have time and a clear view. That is why they are as useful on a Sunday walk as on a week-long trip.

Read them at home. Then you remember them when the wind is blowing.
